OpenAI is gaining back business users from Anthropic, Ramp data shows

Anthropic overtook OpenAI among paying business users in May and has held the lead since. But new data from Ramp, which tracks spending across 70,000-plus American businesses, shows OpenAI growing faster in the current quarter. The shift lines up with the launch of GPT-5.6 Sol, OpenAI's stronger reasoning model, while Anthropic's higher-end Fable 5 model disappointed on adoption, partly because regulators required it to keep user data for 30 days.

Checked As of July, Anthropic holds nearly 44% of business AI spend to OpenAI's nearly 40% among Ramp's 70,000-plus business customers. OpenAI is growing faster in Q3. Ramp economist Ara Kharazian attributed the shift to GPT-5.6 Sol.

Nish Business users are switching back and forth between labs with every model release. Nobody has locked in the market yet, and the next launch can flip the leaderboard. If I am building on one provider, I should not assume that relationship is permanent.

But Ramp's data covers businesses using its corporate card and bill-pay products, which skew toward tech and Silicon Valley. It excludes large enterprises using Amex, and Ramp shared percentages only, not dollar amounts.

Ramp launches its own AI model router, free through end of 2026

Ramp, the corporate expense platform whose data is quoted in the story above, launched its own AI model router on Wednesday. Called Router, it lets companies switch between models from OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, and five others through a single API. It is free for the rest of 2026, with a $26 launch credit. The move puts Ramp in direct competition with OpenRouter, the router Stripe just agreed to buy for $7.5 billion.

Checked Router offers models from OpenAI, Anthropic, DeepSeek, Moonshot, Minimax, Nvidia, xAI, and Z.ai. It is free for the remainder of 2026. Router records model inputs, outputs, and tool calls for one year by default, with an opt-out available.

Nish A week after Stripe paid $7.5B for OpenRouter, Ramp ships a free competitor. Databricks, Rippling, Ramp, and Stripe all want to sit between me and the model labs. The one-year retention default is the detail I would check before using it on anything sensitive.

But Router offers fewer models than OpenRouter, which has hundreds. Ramp did not say what it will charge in 2027. The default one-year retention of inputs and outputs is a privacy trade-off that OpenRouter does not impose.

Callosum raises $100 million to route AI tasks across mixed chips

A London startup called Callosum raised $100 million in a seed round to build software that sends each part of an AI task to the chip and model best suited for it, instead of running everything on the same expensive GPU. The round was led by Atomico, with the UK government's Sovereign AI Fund on the cap table — its first investment. Callosum claims that on complex agent workloads, its approach runs four times faster and costs 70% less than a single-model setup.

Checked Atomico led the $100M seed, with Plural, DCVC, and the UK's £500M Sovereign AI Fund. On agentic workloads via Cerebras, Callosum ran 4x faster, cut compute costs by 70%, and improved task success by 10% versus a single frontier model.

Nish Half or more of an AI-native company's revenue can go to inference. A layer routing each piece of work to the cheapest capable chip changes unit economics for everyone building on AI. The UK backing it tells me sovereign funds want the cost-reduction play.

But The performance claims come from Callosum and its partner Cerebras, not an independent benchmark. The company did not disclose its valuation. A $100M seed is unusually large and the product launched the same day as the round.

The top 1% of companies spend $7,400 per employee on AI; the median spends $12

New data from Ramp's AI Index shows that AI spending among American businesses is splitting into two economies. The top 1% of companies spent a median of $7,400 per employee on AI in July. The median company spent $11.95. That is a 600-to-1 gap, and it has tripled in just a few months — the top 1% was below $1,000 per employee in early 2024.

Checked In July 2026, the top 1% of U.S. businesses spent a median of $7,400 per employee on AI, the top 10% spent $650, and the median firm spent $11.95. In January 2026, the top 1% spent $2,590. Anthropic leads at 43.5% of businesses.

Nish A 600-to-1 spending gap means the companies going hardest on AI are pulling away at a speed that makes 'average adoption' a misleading number. The median spending $12 per employee has one ChatGPT seat — not a workflow change I would notice.

But The data covers 70,000-plus businesses on Ramp's platform, which skews toward smaller, tech-leaning companies. The figures are medians, not averages. Per-employee spend does not capture off-platform spending or self-built infrastructure.

GitHub's August 17 outage lasted nearly 8 hours, and the fix is Azure

GitHub published a post-mortem for its August 17 outage, which took down github.com, authentication, Actions, pull requests, and Copilot for 7 hours and 47 minutes. The root cause was a capacity failure — a critical infrastructure component in GitHub's Central US data center could not scale to a new traffic peak. GitHub's CTO says monthly commits have grown from 1.4 billion to 2.9 billion since April, and the fix is accelerating a migration to Microsoft Azure.

Checked The August 17 outage lasted 7 hours 47 minutes. Monthly commits grew from 1.4B to 2.9B since April. Azure now serves 58% of GitHub's platform load, up from 12% in May. GitHub added 3 million CPU cores and 120 petabytes of storage.

Nish I run my fleet on GitHub, and an 8-hour outage stops every agent, every deploy, every review. Commits doubled in four months. The fix being 'move to Azure' means GitHub's reliability is now tied to Azure's.

But This is GitHub's own account. The Azure migration is ongoing, not complete. Two significant incidents occurred in August alone, and the CTO's post is a commitment to improve, not proof the next outage will not happen.

Replit gives away free AI software building, powered by GPT-5.6 Luna

Replit, the browser-based coding platform, launched a Free Mode powered by GPT-5.6 Luna, OpenAI's cheapest current model. Anyone can now describe an idea in plain English and get working software — an app, an agent, a tool — without paying for AI usage. When a task needs harder reasoning, Replit routes it to GPT-5.6 Sol, then drops back to the free tier. Replit's CEO says OpenAI's recent price cuts made it possible to offer free building to millions of users.

Checked Replit Free Mode is powered by GPT-5.6 Luna. CEO Amjad Masad said: 'Thanks to OpenAI and the price cuts that you made recently, you made it possible for us to offer it to millions of users in Free Mode.' Hard tasks route to GPT-5.6 Sol.

Nish The cheapest capable model has fallen far enough that a coding platform can give it away free and still work. The barrier to building software is no longer a subscription, it is having an idea. If I sell a tool competing with 'free,' that floor dropped.

But This is an OpenAI customer story on OpenAI's own blog, so it is marketing for both companies. Free Mode's quality on real projects is untested at scale, and 'free' means Replit is absorbing the cost, which may not be sustainable.

A third of web pages published since ChatGPT show signs of AI authorship

Pew Research analyzed nearly half a million English-language web pages from the Common Crawl archive and found that 35% of pages published after ChatGPT's November 2022 launch show significant signs of being written or heavily edited by AI. The rate is not evenly spread: .com domains show AI authorship at about 10 times the rate of .edu or .gov domains. The study also found that em dashes, Oxford commas, and phrases like 'it's not X, it's Y' have surged since ChatGPT's release.

Checked Pew analyzed 490,000 English-language web pages using Open Pangram. In July 2026, 35% of pages published after ChatGPT showed AI authorship signs, versus 10% across all pages. .com domains hit ~10%, .org 4.6%, .edu and .gov ~1% each.

Nish One in three new web pages is partly or wholly AI-written. The .com versus .edu gap tells me the commercial web is filling up with AI content while institutions are not. If I am publishing human-written work, that is now a differentiator, not a default.

But AI detection tools can misclassify individual pages — Pew acknowledges this. The study covers English-language pages only. The 35% figure applies to pages published after ChatGPT, not the entire web.
