AI & agentsMartin Alderson

Picking models on speed, not intelligence

Alderson argues the frontier is now good enough at ordinary daily work that throughput decides which model you actually reach for. He breaks an agent turn into its parts to show where the waiting really goes, and why a much faster model does not produce a proportionally faster loop.

Checked His breakdown puts a 50 tok/s agent turn at 65 seconds — 40s inference, 15s tool calls, 10s review — while 250 tok/s only reaches 33 seconds. A 5x model speedup buys a 2x turn.

Nish I route my fleet on capability first, and this is the sharpest argument against me: at 5% of Opus pricing, GLM5.2 finishes turns that Opus never gets around to starting.

But He is benchmarking his own feel, not a task suite. Nothing here shows the cheap fast model finishing the work — only finishing the tokens.

02
Build & shipChris Morgan

Atom beats RSS in the places that actually break

Morgan's case is not about elegance. RSS 2.0 never said whether a description field holds plain text or HTML, so every reader has to guess, and the guess is where real feeds break. Atom's type attribute makes each field declare what it contains.

Checked RSS 2.0 defines no encoding semantics for description or title, and Morgan links a tally of nine mutually incompatible formats shipped under the RSS name.

Nish My own feed.xml is RSS 2.0 and drops the editor note straight into a description field, so this post is about my bug, not someone else's.

But He admits upfront there is no survey or testing behind it, and readers have quietly coped with this ambiguity for twenty years.

03
Build & shipLocalAI

LocalAI rewrites engines in C++ when the install is the problem

The team sets out when it ports an upstream inference stack instead of wrapping it. The trigger is never speed — it is that shipping the original means resolving a Python dependency tree on the user's machine against whatever CUDA and glibc that machine happens to carry.

Checked Installing vLLM produces a 9.1 GiB virtualenv; their vllm.cpp port produces a 66 MiB binary. llama.cpp, whisper.cpp and MLX stay wrapped.

Nish That GiB-to-MiB gap is the failure mode behind half my broken deploys, and LocalAI is refreshingly blunt that the small binary costs CPU throughput.

But Their own figures show SCRFD detection at 0.83x on one thread and 0.69x on eight, so the port is measurably slower where it matters most.

AI & agentsarXiv

A COBOL-to-Java loop that writes its own tests

The paper introduces Locksmith, an iterative loop that synthesises tests for legacy COBOL and its translated Java, then checks that both run identically under deterministic parity. Hacker News retitled this as a story about migrated bugs; the paper is about coverage.

Checked It reports 91.90% branch coverage on an internal production-like COBOL program, across three case studies ranging from 430 to 4,114 source lines.

Nish Three programs topping out at 4,114 lines is a demo rather than a migration, and I would keep it well away from anything that moves money.

But Parity checks only ever cover cases the loop chose to generate. Nothing external audited what it never thought to try.

Build & shipAnkur Sethi

Retyping every generated line by hand

Sethi instructs assistants to show proposed edits in chat rather than apply them, then types each line into his editor himself. His argument is that typing builds a spatial map of where things live, which repays itself later in sharper prompts and earlier detection of bad design.

Checked He states the cost plainly: the habit leaves him "probably only 2x faster" than working with no LLM at all.

Nish Sethi is right that I am not genuinely faster inside repos I never read, though surrendering that much throughput to fix it is a steep price.

But One developer, no measurement behind the 2x, and no evidence that the comprehension he gains survives a month away from the code.

SwiftUI at seven years, from someone still shipping it

A long complaint from a developer who has used the framework since launch. The data-flow model was rebuilt around Observation and still leaves you unable to say why a view re-rendered, while ordinary layout keeps demanding GeometryReader escapes that undo the declarative promise.

Nish A caching API still in beta in July 2026 is the detail that would stop me putting a photo-heavy screen in SwiftUI, seven years into the framework.

But This is one developer's frustration, and the UIKit comparison skips how much boilerplate the old keyboard handling actually demanded.

A benchmark that asks 14 models to draw a frog

The prompt is a frog with a Habsburg jaw, drawn as SVG, three attempts per model per month. The output worth reading is not the pictures but the annotations, where you can watch which systems editorialise about anatomy and mood instead of drawing what was asked.

Checked All 42 runs across 14 models produced a valid SVG in August 2026, and the page publishes no numeric score whatsoever.

Nish A benchmark where every single run passes measures nothing, which is precisely why I would keep it open beside a leaderboard I am tempted to believe.

But Three attempts per model per month is small enough that one odd sample rewrites the narrative, and taste is doing all the grading.

Design & productThe Pudding

Seventy years of the words we teach learners

The Pudding sets the 1953 General Service List against its 2023 replacement, using concreteness ratings and semantic tagging to show what a language's essential vocabulary quietly stopped including as it grew.

Checked The list went from 2,284 to 2,809 words while the most concrete entries fell from 21% of the total to 14%; coverage of general English rose from about 84% to over 90%.

Nish Trading concrete words for wider coverage is exactly the bargain I make writing product copy, and this piece finally shows me the bill.

But Concreteness scores come from a 2014 dataset and the two lists were assembled by different teams for different aims, so part of the shift is method rather than culture.
